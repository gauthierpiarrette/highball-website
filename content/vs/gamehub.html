Highball vs GameSir GameHub

GameHub for Mac is the biggest new entrant in this category. GameSir, the controller company, announced it in February 2026 and has run a free public beta since April. It is real software with real engineering behind it, and far more people have heard of it than have heard of Highball.

The two tools overlap almost completely on who they are for: someone with an Apple Silicon Mac, a Steam library, and no interest in learning what a bottle is. Where they differ is mostly not performance.

What GameHub does better

The first run. GameHub links your Steam account and downloads game depots itself, so no Windows Steam client ever appears on screen. Highball's Steam support is one click, but what that click does is install the Windows Steam client into a bottle and let it update itself, which is slower the first time and puts a Windows window in front of you. For a person who has never used Wine, GameHub's flow is better, and it is not close.

The rest of the list is real too:

  • It is not a reskin. Their public GitHub org carries their own Wine patches, an actively developed fork of DXMT, and a JIT build of the rosettax87 x87 floating-point hack.
  • FSR upscaling and per-game profiles (Wine version, sync mode, video decode) built into the UI.
  • A storefront library with editor's picks, review scores and genre tags. That is a discovery layer Highball does not have.
  • A funded team rather than one person, at a hardware company that has been shipping since 2013.
  • Controller support from the company that makes the controllers.

Performance between the two is broadly a wash, because underneath the branding both are Wine plus some combination of DXVK, DXMT and Apple's D3DMetal. Nobody is doing magic here.

The trust argument, without the theatrics

Community reaction to GameHub is split down the middle. Showcase posts on r/macgaming score in the hundreds of points because the thing works, and the comment sections under those same posts fill up with objections about closed source, where account data ends up, and whether redistributing Apple's D3DMetal is licensed at all. Both halves are honest reactions. People who raise the concerns and people who use it daily are often the same people.

Highball is not going to tell you GameHub is malware, because there is no evidence of that and the accusation is cheap. The difference worth stating is structural. Highball is GPL-3, so you can read what it does. There is no account, so there is nothing about you to store or to leak. There is no telemetry. The download is a notarized DMG you fetch directly instead of a file behind a chat server. And the compatibility claims are published as data rather than made inside a closed app.

That last one is the part nobody else is doing: 101 curated games, 39 marked blocked by kernel anti-cheat, 12,491 ProtonDB-derived predictions, CC0 for the curated set and ODbL for the predictions. How the data works.

Where Highball is genuinely behind

It is a few weeks old and it shows. Of those 101 curated entries, exactly 5 have been verified by hand on real Apple Silicon hardware. The rest is inherited or predicted, and predictions come from Linux results, which is a different graphics stack.

The renderer matrix is a structure with mostly empty rows. Highball can switch a bottle between DXVK, DXMT and D3DMetal, and which one works varies a lot per game, but the database currently holds zero games with two dated renderer verdicts to compare. That is the gap, stated plainly, and filling it is the point of the whole project.

Highball also has no depot pipeline, no discovery shelves, and no upscaler of its own. On raw feature count GameHub is ahead today.

What neither one fixes

Kernel anti-cheat. Vanguard, Ricochet and the kernel modes of EAC and BattlEye load a Windows kernel driver, and there is no Windows kernel under either app. That is architecture, not a missing feature. Userspace cases vary per title, which is why the database records a verdict per game rather than per anti-cheat vendor: Helldivers 2, for instance, is playable under CrossOver despite carrying anti-cheat, as is Elden Ring's single-player. The full explanation is here.

Neither tool covers GOG or Ubisoft Connect properly. Both are Apple Silicon only.

Which should you use

Pick GameHub if you want the shortest path from a Mac to a running Steam game, you do not mind making an account and joining a Discord to get the installer, and closed source is not a dealbreaker for you. It is the better first-run experience today and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Pick Highball if you want to see what the software does, keep your accounts out of it, or check a game's odds before spending 80 GB and an evening on it. It suits people who want the renderer control rather than a profile chosen for them, and people who would rather read a public database than trust an in-app badge.

Do not pick Highball if you have an Intel Mac, if you want a curated storefront to browse, or if you need today's smoothest Steam setup and you are fine with the trade that buys it. And if you would rather pay a company that employs Wine developers, buy CrossOver instead. That comparison is here.
